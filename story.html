The outlier in the pack

Why the dangerous signal in battery data is never the average — it is the handful of packs that ran hot when they had no reason to. And why finding them means keeping every raw sample, not rolling it up.

The problem

An average is where a battery fault goes to hide

Every EV carries a Battery Management System that reports per-cell temperature, voltage, and pack current about ten times a second. A fleet of thousands of cars streams those signals back for field-quality analysis. The question that matters is simple to ask and hard to answer: which individual packs run hotter than they should?

A cell that climbs to 70 °C for eight seconds during a fast charge is a thermal event — a precursor to accelerated aging, capacity loss, and, at the extreme, thermal runaway. It is also almost invisible. Averaged across a day of driving, across a pack of cells, across a fleet, those eight seconds vanish into a comfortable mid-thirties number that looks perfectly healthy.

The whole fleet looks fine at 38 °C. The truth — a 70 °C excursion on three cars — lives in a fraction of a percent of the data. You cannot aggregate your way to it. You have to keep the raw signal and go hunting.

That single constraint — outliers survive only at raw resolution — is what shapes every plot below, and it is what separates a tool that streams raw detail on demand from one that must pre-summarize to stay responsive.

Reading the plots

Three ways to make an outlier show itself

The same fault looks different depending on the axes you plot it against. Each view answers a different question about a suspect pack — and each one falls apart the moment you average it.

VIEW A · time domain

Overlaid raw curves — temperature against time-in-trip

Every car's raw temperature trace for a trip, drawn on top of every other car's. Thousands of near-identical curves stack into a dense ribbon: the healthy fleet, warming gently as the trip goes on. A faulty pack draws a line that peels up and out of that ribbon — a repeating spike every few minutes as a weak cell overheats under load.

X axis
seconds into the trip
Y axis
cell temperature (°C)
Outlier
a curve whose shape breaks from the ribbon — recurring spikes past 55 °C
Why aggregation kills it: a percentile band (median ± p95) is the only way a summarized tool can draw this many curves. The band shows the fleet is fine and erases the one curve that isn't — the spike is a rounding error inside the p95 envelope.
VIEW B · state domain

Raw-sample density — temperature against state of charge

Forget time; ask instead at what battery state does the heat appear? Every raw sample is dropped into a fine grid of (state-of-charge × temperature) and colored by how many samples land there. The fleet forms one dense, glowing cloud — normal operation. An outlier is a faint smear of hot pixels floating above the cloud, in a region of the state space no healthy pack should ever visit.

X axis
state of charge (%)
Y axis
cell temperature (°C)
Color
log density of raw samples — how common that combination is
Outlier
faint hot cells in empty state-space above the main cloud
Why aggregation kills it: a coarse hexbin — the summarized version — pools those four faint pixels into a big bin dominated by thousands of normal neighbors. The outlier is averaged into invisibility before it ever reaches the screen.
VIEW C · load domain

Phase portrait — temperature against pack current

The most physical view. Plot temperature against the current flowing in or out of the pack, and each drive-and-charge cycle traces a loop: heat rises under load, falls when the load eases. A healthy pack's loops are tight and cool. A failing pack's loop bulges upward — the same current produces far more heat, the signature of rising internal resistance in an aging or damaged cell.

X axis
pack current (A) — negative under discharge
Y axis
cell temperature (°C)
Outlier
a loop that bulges hot — excess heat per amp, i.e. rising resistance
Why aggregation kills it: a loop is an ordered path through thousands of raw points. Summarize the points and the path — the very thing that carries the diagnosis — is gone. There is no "average" of a trajectory.
What it means for the battery

An outlier is a physical story, not a red dot

Each way a pack leaves the healthy population points to a specific failure mode. Reading the outlier is reading the battery.

Recurring thermal spikes

A weak or high-resistance cell dumps heat under load. Repeated excursions past 55 °C accelerate electrolyte breakdown and SEI growth — permanent capacity loss, and a runaway risk if it compounds.

Heat at the wrong state of charge

Getting hot near full charge or during fast charge, when a healthy pack stays cool, points to lithium plating or a failing cooling loop — both irreversible and both safety-relevant.

A bulging phase loop

More heat per amp means internal resistance is climbing. That is the fingerprint of an aging cell and the leading indicator of end-of-life — the warranty-cost signal.

Depressed cell voltage

The hot cars in the fleet also sag ~50 mV lower under load. Heat and voltage sag together confirm a genuine cell defect rather than a stray sensor glitch.

Beyond batteries

Every tested product has this shape of data

The battery is one instance of a general pattern: a high-frequency signal from a fleet of units in the field, where the fault is a rare deviation that no summary preserves. The same dashboard — brush the population, drill to the raw member — applies wherever a product is instrumented and tested.

  • Electric motors & inverters
    winding temp · torque · vibration

    A bearing starting to fail shows as a vibration signature seconds long — invisible in an RMS average, obvious in the raw trace.

  • Combustion powertrain
    oil pressure · knock · coolant temp

    A knock event or a pressure dropout is a transient. The engine's daily average looks nominal right up until the failure.

  • Industrial & wind turbines
    gearbox temp · blade load · rpm

    Fatigue accumulates in load cycles. The phase-portrait view is the native language of rotating machinery health.

  • Medical & lab devices
    pump flow · pressure · dose

    A single out-of-spec dose cycle among millions is exactly the raw-resolution outlier a regulator will ask you to produce on demand.

Wherever the answer to "which unit is failing, and why" lives in the raw signal and not the summary, the tool has to reach the raw signal — fast, on demand, at fleet scale. That is the capability this dashboard is built to demonstrate.
