Reading the fleet, down to one cell

A three-step drill: from a picture of the whole fleet, to a sampled group of suspects, to the raw signal of a single vehicle — without ever pre-summarizing away the outlier you are hunting. Here is how the workflow reads, and why each step is hard or impossible in the classic BI tools.

The workflow

Three plots, three altitudes

You descend from the aggregate to the individual. Each step narrows the question and increases the resolution of the data you are looking at.

1

The aggregated picture — a density map of the whole fleet

plot: 2D histogram · X = state of charge · Y = cell temperature · color = log(sample count)

Every raw sample from every vehicle — millions of points — is dropped into a fine grid of (state-of-charge × temperature) and colored by how many samples land in each cell. The fleet forms one dense, glowing cloud: normal operation. The eye is drawn to what is not in the cloud — faint hot cells floating above it, where a few packs reached temperatures no healthy battery should at that charge state. This is the full-resolution view: nothing is averaged, so a rare outlier still leaves its mark.

2

The sampled suspects — raw curves, overlaid

control: sample-size slider (default 30) · plot: cell temp vs time, one raw line per vehicle · table: forensic detail

The rectangle you drew is turned into a query. Rather than pull every vehicle in the region (there may be hundreds), a sample-size slider takes the N hottest of them — 30 by default — and fetches their raw temperature-vs-time traces from the lakehouse on demand. Overlaid, the healthy curves sit low and flat; a failing pack’s line peels upward in repeating spikes. Beside the plot, a table lists each sampled vehicle with its peak temperature, seconds spent hot, mileage, and lowest voltage — sortable evidence, worst first.

3

One vehicle, in full — the detailed line plot

action: click a table row · plot: that vehicle’s complete raw signal at full 10 Hz

Click a row in the suspects table and the dashboard fetches every raw sample for that one vehicle — a hundred thousand points — and draws its full-resolution trace. This is the forensic close-up: the exact shape of each thermal excursion, when it happened, how long it lasted, how the pack recovered. It is the evidence you would attach to a warranty claim or a field-quality report, and it exists only because the raw signal was never thrown away.

The constraint that shapes everything

Why the raw signal has to stay raw

The fault is a rare, seconds-long deviation. Averaging — over time, over cells, over the fleet — is exactly what erases it. So the analysis must reach the raw signal at every step, fast, at fleet scale. That single requirement is where the classic BI tools struggle.

A BI tool that must load a resident extract or in-memory model, and can only render a few thousand marks, is structurally the wrong shape for “find the individual outlier in a billion raw points.” It has to summarize to stay responsive — and the summary is where the outlier dies.

The product · what happened to the battery

What the plots are telling you about the pack

The signal is cell temperature. To read the outlier is to read a physical failure inside the battery. Here is what the pack is, where the number comes from, and what has gone wrong in the cell that runs hot.

1 · Where the number comes from — pack → module → cell

In the data: this is cell_temp_max on the Y axis of every plot. A single failing cell, buried among thousands, is what pushes it above the healthy band.

2 · What went wrong inside the cell — healthy vs failing

In the data: that hot spot is the seconds-long temperature spike you see in Step 2’s overlaid curves and Step 3’s detailed trace. It repeats every time the pack is worked hard.

3 · Why it runs hot — same current, more heat

In the data: this is why an outlier can run hot at a normal state of charge and load — the density map (Step 1) places it high on the temperature axis even where healthy cars sit cool, because the heat comes from the defect, not from how hard the car is being driven.
Tool by tool, step by step

The same three steps in Tableau, Power BI and Qlik

All three are strong BI tools. The gap here is narrow and specific. We take each workflow step in turn. For each tool, one short line says what happens and why — traceable back to how that tool is built.

works partial / degraded not possible cleanly

Step 1

Show the full-resolution density of every raw sample

Databricks App✓

Bins every raw sample in the lakehouse. The outlier cell is drawn.

Tableau✕

Bins into a coarse hexbin to stay fast. The lone hot cell is pooled away.

Power BI✕

Caps a scatter at ~3.5k points (~10k sampled). It drops the rare cell.

Qlik✕

Chart engine caps points too. At fleet scale it aggregates the cell away.

Step 2 · the lasso

Turn the rectangle into a fetch of raw rows from the source

Databricks App✓

The box becomes a WHERE query. It pulls raw rows on demand.

Tableau~

The lasso only selects marks already in the extract. It cannot fetch new raw rows.

Power BI✕

No native brush that pushes a predicate to the source. Filtering runs on the loaded model.

Qlik~

Selection is great, but only over data already loaded in memory. Not a lakehouse fetch.

Step 2 · the overlay

Overlay the sampled vehicles’ raw curves, one line each

Databricks App✓

Draws each sampled vehicle’s raw trace. The failing curve’s shape is visible.

Tableau✕

Too many marks. It falls back to a percentile band. The individual shape is lost.

Power BI✕

Point cap plus line aggregation. A true raw overlay is not possible.

Qlik✕

Same rendering ceiling. Hundreds of raw curves cannot be drawn honestly.

Step 3

Drill to one vehicle’s full 10 Hz raw trace (~100k points)

Databricks App✓

Fetches that one vehicle’s every raw point on click. Nothing pre-loaded.

Tableau~

Works only if those rows were extracted up front. The extract then balloons.

Power BI~

DirectQuery can fetch the vehicle, but the visual still caps and thins the points.

Qlik~

The raw points must be in the in-memory model first, and the chart still caps them.

The one root cause. Tableau, Power BI and Qlik all keep granular data resident — a Hyper extract, an imported model, an in-memory table — and can render only a few thousand marks. So they must summarize to stay fast, and their lasso works over what is already loaded. The App keeps the data in the lakehouse: it renders a full-resolution aggregate, and every lasso is a fresh bounded query that fetches only the raw rows that step needs.

Why the App stays flat as the fleet grows. Its overview reads a small pre-computed aggregate, and each lasso is a bounded pushdown query — it touches only the sampled vehicles’ rows (helped by clustering the raw table on vehicle id), never the whole dataset. Add ten times the vehicles and the interaction cost barely moves, because the query is always bounded and the full data never travels to the client.

Why the BI tools degrade. Their responsiveness depends on holding granular data resident. As the fleet grows the extract / in-memory model grows with it: the initial load and every refresh get slower, memory pressure rises, and to keep interactions bearable the tool summarizes harder — which, for outlier work, is the one thing you cannot afford. DirectQuery trades the big load for a per-interaction round-trip to the source, moving the latency into every click instead of removing it.
